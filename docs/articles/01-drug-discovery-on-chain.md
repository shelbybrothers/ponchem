# Drug discovery you can check: Ponchem and the on-chain lab

Finding a new medicine starts long before a pill, a trial or a lab bench. It starts with a question a computer can answer: does this small molecule fit into the pocket of this protein, and how tightly does it hold on? Computer-aided drug design, often shortened to CADD, answers that question millions of times so chemists spend their time and money on the few molecules worth making. Docking places a molecule inside a protein's binding site, turns and bends it into thousands of shapes, and scores each one. The best score is an estimate of binding free energy, the Gibbs energy released when the molecule settles in. More negative is better. Ponchem takes that everyday step of drug research and puts it where anyone can run it, and anyone can check it.

The field has changed fast. Machine learning now predicts protein structures, suggests new molecules and ranks candidates at a scale no team could manage by hand. That is what people usually mean by AI drug discovery. But the loudest progress has a quiet weakness: most results can't be reproduced by the reader. A score in a slide deck comes from one lab's software, one lab's settings and one lab's files. You are asked to trust it. Ponchem starts from the opposite end. Every number it shows is produced by a scoring function anyone can read, run on inputs anyone can download, and recorded where nobody can quietly change it.

The inputs are real. Every one of the 100 cancer targets in the library is an experimental X-ray structure from the RCSB Protein Data Bank, the public archive that has held the world's protein structures for more than fifty years. Each one comes from a human protein with a known drug or inhibitor bound in its pocket, which is how the site knows where the pocket is. They cover twenty cancer groups: lung, breast, colorectal, leukemia, lymphoma, pancreatic, liver, brain and more. Each target page names its PDB id, its resolution and the co-crystal ligand that defines its pocket, and links straight to its entry on rcsb.org.

The molecules are chosen with care too. The library holds 152 bioactive compounds, most of them from plants: quercetin from onions and capers, curcumin from turmeric, berberine from barberry, resveratrol from grapes, and camptothecin, the parent of two approved cancer drugs. Plants have given medicine many of its most important scaffolds, and these compounds are ones people know by name. Their 3D shapes come from the RCSB Chemical Component Dictionary or from PubChem, and every ligand page says which. Picking a compound from a plant you grew up with and docking it into a kinase that drives a cancer is a real computational question, and here anyone can ask it.

The docking runs in your browser. Ponchem searches for a pose with a seeded Monte Carlo search that turns the whole molecule and twists its rotatable bonds, then scores each pose with an empirical function from the AutoDock Vina family. Its five weighted terms describe steric contact, repulsion, hydrophobic contact and hydrogen bonding, and they come from the weights Trott and Olson fitted on about 1,300 known complexes. The engine is an independent implementation, written in integers so it gives the same answer on every machine. You watch the molecule settle into the pocket in 3D while it searches. You can keep the quick, standard or deep presets, or write your own docking method as JSON: temperature, move sizes, restarts, rigid or flexible ligand. You can share a method by link and reuse it later.

The part that is new is the docking test. When you are happy with a pose, you send it to the PonchemLab contract on Robinhood Chain. The contract does not accept a score. It accepts atom positions, checks that they are a legal shape of that molecule (bond lengths, bond angles, no atoms crashing into each other, every atom inside the pocket), and then computes the score itself with the same integer function. The number on the site after that is the chain's number. You cannot claim a good result without a pose that earns it. Every test is stored with its wallet, its pose and the method that produced it, so anyone can rebuild it and score it again with the published reference code.

A test costs 0.0001 ETH or 100 $PONCHEM, and the payer chooses. That small price keeps the record meaningful: every entry cost someone something to make. Tests feed a live research report that ranks, for each of the top-threat cancers, the best target and compound pairs by binding free energy, with estimated potency (pKd and Kd), ligand efficiency, run counts and the number of distinct researchers behind them. The report downloads as Markdown and prints cleanly. Sponsors can put ETH into a target's prize pool, and each week the wallet with the best test on that target takes it. A pool turns attention into incentive: fund the targets you care about, and the community screens them.

Research is a social process, so Ponchem is too. Every docking test has its own page with the 3D pose, the chain's score, the geometry checks and the method used. Other researchers can review it on chain with one to five stars and a short note. You can set a researcher name, so your tests say who did them, and post any result to X in one click. The dashboard turns steady work into progress: levels from Observer to Lab Head, and badges for your first test, ten targets, a strong binder or an epoch won. Each test page can also carry an AI analysis, written by the model you pick, that explains the result in plain words. It is always labelled as the model's text and never treated as evidence.

It is worth being clear about what this is and is not. A docking score is an estimate from a rigid protein and a simplified energy model. It ranks candidates for further study. It does not show that a compound works in a cell, an animal or a person, and nothing on Ponchem claims otherwise. What it offers is a well-defined, repeatable first step, shared openly: a public screening campaign anyone can join, whose every result anyone can check, and that no single party controls. The hard biology still happens in laboratories. Ponchem makes sure the computational groundwork that points them somewhere is honest, visible and shared.

Ponchem is a Pons Lab CADD project, part of the ponsfamily.com family, built on structures from the RCSB Protein Data Bank and running on Robinhood Chain. Open the lab at ponchem.ai, pick a target and a compound, and watch the pose settle. Run a docking test to put your result on chain, read what others have found in the research report, and follow the work at x.com/PonchemAI. Computer-aided drug design, scored on chain.
